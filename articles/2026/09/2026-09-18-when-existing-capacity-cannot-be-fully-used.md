# When Existing Capacity Cannot Be Fully Used

**Focus:** Capacity • Institutions • Coordination • Infrastructure • Access • Utilization

## Public Question

**Why can a system appear short of capacity even when some capacity already exists?**

Capacity is often treated as a physical question.

How many transmission lines exist?

How many railway tracks are available?

How large is the port?

How many machines can a factory operate?

Physical infrastructure matters.

But the existence of an asset does not mean that every potential user can access it, that operators can allocate it efficiently, or that available capacity can be fully used under the conditions required.

This creates an important distinction.

A system can face a capacity problem even when some physical capacity still exists.

The constraint may be physical.

It may also arise from access rules, planning procedures, information gaps, incompatible operating arrangements, scheduling, or insufficient coordination among institutions.

Recognizing the difference matters because the two problems can require different responses.

## Physical Capacity Is Only the First State

Consider an electricity network.

The cables, substations, transformers, and other assets determine part of the physical limit of the system.

But a new electricity producer or industrial customer does not gain access merely because a grid exists nearby.

The operator must determine whether connection is technically possible.

Network conditions must be assessed.

Capacity may need to be allocated.

Operating requirements must be satisfied.

Information about connection conditions must reach potential users.

A project can therefore sit near physical infrastructure without having immediate access to usable network capacity.

The same distinction appears in transport.

A railway line may physically exist between two countries.

That does not mean every train operator can obtain a suitable path at the time required.

Track access must be planned and coordinated with passenger services, freight trains, maintenance works, stations, terminals, and neighboring infrastructure managers.

The physical asset creates potential capacity.

Institutions and operating systems influence how much of that potential becomes practically usable.

## Europe Is Confronting This Problem In Electricity Networks

The European Commission identifies grid connections as a significant challenge for the European electricity system.

Its current grid material states that connection queues were present in at least 16 European Union countries as of mid 2026.

The Commission identifies multiple causes, including inadequate planning and delays in network development, limited transparency concerning available capacity and locational incentives, and connection procedures that do not always account adequately for project maturity.

These causes are not equivalent.

Some indicate a physical constraint.

A local grid may lack sufficient infrastructure to accommodate additional demand or generation.

Others concern how existing capacity is identified, allocated, and accessed.

That distinction changes the type of response required.

## Six Capacity States

A useful way to understand the problem is to separate six conditions.

### Physical Capacity

The infrastructure exists and has a defined technical capability.

A transmission line, railway track, terminal, pipeline, warehouse, or production facility forms part of the physical asset base.

Physical capacity does not establish how much capacity is currently available.

### Available Capacity

Part of the physical capability is currently capable of being used.

Maintenance, outages, safety restrictions, technical conditions, network conditions, or other operating requirements can reduce the amount available at a particular time.

Physical capacity and available capacity are therefore not necessarily identical.

### Accessible Capacity

Available capacity can be reached and allocated to the user that requires it.

This can depend on connection procedures, access rights, technical compatibility, scheduling, contracts, permits, or allocation rules.

Capacity can exist and be operational while remaining inaccessible to a particular user under the required conditions.

### Utilized Capacity

Accessible capacity is actually being used.

A railway path can be allocated but unused.

A factory can contain operational machinery while running below its potential output.

An electrical connection can exist while actual demand remains below the permitted level.

Utilization therefore describes how much accessible capacity is actually performing work.

### Coordination Constraint

Some usable capability may exist, but information, scheduling, institutional arrangements, access procedures, or interactions among operators can prevent it from being used as effectively as it otherwise could be.

The constraint is not necessarily the absence of infrastructure.

It concerns how access to that infrastructure is organized.

### Physical Constraint

The infrastructure itself does not provide sufficient technical capability to meet the required demand under the relevant operating conditions.

Better coordination may improve utilization around the constraint.

It cannot by itself create physical infrastructure that does not exist.

These states should not be treated as equivalent.

## Connection Queues Show Why The Distinction Matters

Suppose ten projects request access to an electricity network.

If the relevant network can technically accommodate only five under the required conditions, the system contains a physical capacity constraint.

Additional lines, substations, transformers, storage, flexibility resources, or other physical changes may be required depending on network conditions.

But consider another situation.

Some projects in the queue may not yet be ready to build.

Some may have changed their plans.

Some requested capacity may not be required within the expected timeframe.

Information about available capacity may be incomplete.

Connection procedures may not adequately distinguish projects at different stages of maturity.

In that situation, part of the apparent shortage can also involve allocation and connection management.

This is why European Commission guidance on efficient and timely grid connections includes recommendations concerning transparency, project readiness, monitoring and cleaning of connection queues, flexible connection arrangements, and more efficient use of existing grids.

The guidance does not claim that procedural improvement eliminates the need for grid investment.

It addresses how existing infrastructure can be used more effectively while network development continues.

## Better Information Can Improve Capacity Use

Information does not create another transmission line.

But incomplete information can make it more difficult to match projects with available network conditions.

Imagine that an industrial company is evaluating locations for a new facility.

One location has very limited electricity connection capability.

Another has greater available capacity.

If information about those conditions becomes available only late in project development, the company may have already advanced a project at a location where the network is heavily constrained.

More transparent capacity information can allow network conditions to be considered earlier.

The physical grid has not changed.

The ability to coordinate potential demand with existing infrastructure has improved.

This can improve practical use of existing capacity.

It is not additional installed capacity.

## Railways Provide Another Example

Europe's rail network shows a similar distinction between physical infrastructure and the systems used to allocate it.

Railway tracks are expensive physical assets.

Different passenger and freight operators require access to the same network.

Infrastructure also requires capacity for maintenance.

Cross border services can move through networks managed by multiple infrastructure managers.

The European Union adopted Regulation (EU) 2026/1184 on 20 May 2026 concerning the use of railway infrastructure capacity in the single European railway area.

The Regulation establishes a capacity management framework covering strategic planning, scheduling and allocation, adaptation of allocated capacity, coordination, and management of infrastructure restrictions.

It also requires infrastructure managers to provide accurate and current information concerning capacity availability throughout the capacity management process.

The European Commission describes the previous capacity management system as largely national, annual, and dependent on manual procedures.

The new framework is intended to strengthen coordination and improve the use of increasingly congested railway infrastructure, including cross border operations.

The railway tracks do not become longer because planning improves.

The objective is to obtain more useful service from existing infrastructure through better capacity management.

## A Timetable Is Part Of Usable Capacity

A railway system does not consist only of tracks.

It also depends on time.

Two trains cannot necessarily occupy the same section of track at the same moment.

Maintenance requires access to the network.

Fast passenger trains and slower freight trains can interact with one another.

International services must be coordinated across several networks.

Railway capacity is therefore partly a scheduling problem.

Physical infrastructure creates the space through which trains can move.

Capacity management determines how that space is allocated across time.

Fragmented coordination can therefore reduce the practical service obtained from the available infrastructure.

Improved scheduling cannot create unlimited capacity.

It can help improve the use of capacity that already exists.

## Access Is Different From Availability

Another important distinction is between available capacity and accessible capacity.

Imagine a port with an unused terminal.

The terminal may physically exist.

It may be technically operational.

But a particular shipper may still be unable to use it because the required berth schedule, equipment, customs arrangements, inland connections, contracts, or technical services are unavailable.

The physical capability is present.

It is not necessarily accessible for that transaction.

The same principle can apply to electricity networks, railways, warehouses, telecommunications, production facilities, and other shared infrastructure.

Availability asks:

**Can the relevant capacity operate?**

Access asks:

**Can the required user actually use it under the necessary conditions?**

Those are different questions.

## Utilization Adds Another Layer

Even accessible capacity may remain unused.

A factory may have ten functioning production lines but operate only seven because demand is insufficient.

A rail corridor may have available paths at times that freight operators cannot use economically because terminals or customer facilities operate on different schedules.

A warehouse may have empty storage space that is unsuitable for a particular product.

Unused capacity therefore does not automatically mean readily usable capacity.

The relevant question is why the capacity is unused.

The answer may involve:

• demand

• operating cost

• technical compatibility

• access

• timing

• coordination

Only after identifying the constraint can the system determine whether additional construction addresses the relevant problem.

## Coordination Can Improve Effective Utilization

Where the binding constraint involves organization, information, access, or scheduling rather than insufficient physical capability, better coordination can improve effective utilization without immediately constructing another asset.

Examples can include:

• clearer information about available grid connections

• better coordination of railway paths across networks

• coordinated maintenance planning

• improved scheduling between ports and inland transport

• review of connection capacity associated with projects that no longer satisfy applicable readiness requirements

• flexible access arrangements where technically and legally appropriate

These measures do not produce unlimited capacity.

They can reduce situations in which existing infrastructure remains difficult to use because the surrounding systems are poorly aligned.

The European Commission describes more efficient grid management, improved connection procedures, flexible connection agreements, and better planning as measures that can support more effective use of existing networks while electricity infrastructure expansion continues.

## Institutions Are Part Of Infrastructure Performance

Physical infrastructure is operated through institutions and organizations.

Someone determines who can connect.

Someone schedules access.

Someone maintains the asset.

Someone publishes relevant information.

Someone establishes technical requirements.

Someone manages competing requests for limited capacity.

This does not mean institutions can manufacture physical capacity through administration.

It means infrastructure performance depends partly on how these functions are carried out.

A technically capable network with fragmented operating arrangements may deliver less practical service than its physical capability alone suggests.

Better coordinated arrangements can sometimes improve useful output from the same physical infrastructure.

Infrastructure performance therefore depends on both assets and organization.

## Coordination Constraints Should Not Be Confused With Physical Scarcity

This distinction matters because different constraints require different responses.

If a transformer is operating at its applicable technical limit and additional demand exceeds its capability, the physical constraint is real.

Administrative improvement alone cannot create additional electrical capacity.

If a railway section is operating at its practical capacity during the periods when demand exists, scheduling improvements may have limited ability to accommodate additional trains.

Physical expansion may eventually be required.

But where usable capacity remains inaccessible because scheduling, information, or access arrangements are poorly aligned, constructing another asset without addressing those conditions may leave part of the original utilization problem unresolved.

A useful sequence is:

**Identify the capacity state → Identify the constraint → Improve access and coordination where appropriate → Add physical capacity where required**

Coordination and investment can complement one another.

They are not interchangeable.

## Building More Can Still Be Necessary

Europe's electricity network demonstrates both sides of the problem.

The European Commission identifies insufficient network capacity, delays in network development, and growing connection queues among current grid challenges.

Its current grid policy combines measures intended to accelerate infrastructure development with measures intended to improve the use of existing networks.

The existence of coordination opportunities does not establish that Europe already has sufficient grid infrastructure.

Where physical limits are binding, additional infrastructure remains necessary.

Likewise, improved railway capacity management does not eliminate congested sections that may ultimately require physical expansion.

Institutional improvement can help unlock usable capacity that already exists.

It cannot create physical capacity that has not been built.

## Capacity Problems Can Contain Several Constraints

Real systems can contain several constraints simultaneously.

A region may require additional electricity infrastructure while also facing inefficient allocation of existing connection capacity.

A railway corridor may require new physical capacity while also experiencing fragmented scheduling.

A port may require additional terminal capability while also experiencing coordination constraints among ships, terminals, customs processes, and inland transport.

The observed capacity problem can therefore contain:

**Physical Constraints**

**Institutional Constraints**

**Operational Constraints**

**Information Constraints**

Treating them as one category can hide the actual source of the limitation.

## Existing Capacity Has Value Before New Capacity Arrives

Physical infrastructure often requires significant time to plan, approve, finance, construct, and commission.

Existing capacity therefore matters while additional infrastructure is being developed.

Where usable capacity can be released through improved scheduling, clearer access arrangements, better information, or technically appropriate flexible operation, those changes can support activity before major construction is completed.

This does not reduce the importance of longer term investment.

It separates two questions:

**How can existing infrastructure be used more effectively now?**

and

**What additional infrastructure will be required for future demand?**

Both matter.

## Capacity Is A Chain

The distinction can be summarized as:

**Physical Capacity → Available Capacity → Accessible Capacity → Utilized Capacity**

Capacity can be reduced between each state.

Maintenance, outages, technical restrictions, or safety requirements can reduce physical capability to available capacity.

Access arrangements and technical requirements can prevent available capacity from becoming accessible to a particular user.

Scheduling, demand, compatibility, information, or operating conditions can prevent accessible capacity from becoming utilized.

The size of the physical asset therefore does not describe the entire usable capacity state.

The system must also understand what limits each transition.

## SINTELRA Note

Capacity shortages are not always simple shortages of physical assets.

Sometimes infrastructure is genuinely constrained.

Additional infrastructure may then be required.

Sometimes physical capability exists but cannot be reached, allocated, scheduled, or used effectively under the required conditions.

The system then faces a different constraint.

This is why:

**Physical Capacity**

**Available Capacity**

**Accessible Capacity**

**Utilized Capacity**

should remain separate concepts.

Construction expands physical capability.

Coordination influences how effectively existing capability can be accessed and used.

Neither replaces the other.

A strong infrastructure system requires sufficient physical capacity.

It also requires information, access arrangements, operating processes, and institutional coordination capable of converting that capacity into usable service.

Before asking only how much additional infrastructure must be built, another question is therefore useful:

**How much of the infrastructure that already exists can actually be used under the conditions required?**
