# When Public Demand Becomes Industrial Infrastructure

**Focus:** Public Demand • Procurement • Industrial Capacity • Supply Chains • Production • Strategic Infrastructure

## Public Question

**What changes when governments use purchasing power not only to buy products, but also to support the capacity to produce them?**

Government purchasing is usually understood as expenditure.

A public authority needs trains, medical equipment, energy systems, digital services, construction materials, or other goods and services. It runs a procurement process, selects a supplier, pays for what it needs, and receives the resulting product or service.

But procurement can have another effect.

When public demand is sufficiently large, credible, and persistent, it can influence the production system behind the purchase.

A supplier deciding whether to expand a factory does not consider only whether a product is needed. It must also consider whether enough paying demand is likely to exist to justify investment in additional capacity.

In that sense, public demand can become part of the economic infrastructure supporting industrial production.

## Europe Is Reconsidering the Role of the Public Buyer

Public procurement represents a substantial part of economic activity in the European Union.

The European Parliament's 2025 public procurement report described procurement expenditure as approximately 14 percent of European Union gross domestic product.

The report also examined a more strategic role for procurement, including its relationship with resilience, innovation, sustainability, industrial capacity, competition, value for public money, and European preference.

At the same time, it emphasized legal certainty, fair competition, international commitments, and the need to avoid protectionist outcomes.

The European Commission's Clean Industrial Deal also identifies public procurement and other demand measures as potential tools for supporting demand for sustainable and resilient products.

These developments indicate a broader policy question.

Public purchasing is not only about how governments acquire goods and services.

It can also affect the market conditions against which suppliers decide whether to invest.

That does not mean every public contract becomes an industrial policy instrument.

The effect depends on how demand is structured and how suppliers respond.

## Demand Can Change an Investment Decision

Industrial capacity often has to be built before additional products can be delivered at scale.

A manufacturer may need to purchase machinery, expand a plant, hire workers, qualify suppliers, establish production processes, arrange energy supply, or increase logistics capacity.

Those decisions require capital.

They also involve uncertainty.

If expected demand is weak or unpredictable, a company may hesitate to build additional production capacity. A factory expansion that later lacks sufficient orders can become an expensive underused asset.

A credible purchaser can change part of that calculation.

Suppose a manufacturer sees only occasional demand for a particular technology.

Expanding production may be difficult to justify.

If public buyers create sufficiently visible and sustained demand, the producer may have more information against which to evaluate an expansion.

The physical requirements of manufacturing have not changed.

What has changed is the visibility of the market.

The European Commission has applied similar reasoning in the Clean Industrial Deal. That policy communication identifies public procurement and related demand measures as tools that can support demand for sustainable and resilient products and strengthen incentives for industrial investment.

This establishes a potential mechanism.

It does not establish that every procurement programme will produce additional industrial capacity.

## From Purchase to Production System

The structural relationship can be simplified as:

**Public need → Credible demand → Supplier investment → Production capacity → Supply chain development → Deliverable output**

Each stage is different.

A public requirement identifies something that a government wants to purchase.

A procurement process can translate that requirement into market demand.

A contract can provide stronger evidence of demand than a general policy objective.

Supplier investment may then convert expected orders into additional machinery, facilities, workers, processes, or supplier relationships.

Only after those capabilities exist and operate can additional physical output be produced.

This distinction matters.

A procurement policy is not installed manufacturing capacity.

A tender is not a factory.

A contract can support demand visibility, but production still has to be financed, built, organised, and operated.

Industrial capacity appears only when the required physical and organisational system exists.

## Scale and Predictability Change the Signal

Not every government purchase has the same industrial effect.

A small or isolated order may have little influence on investment decisions.

The potential influence becomes greater when several conditions are present.

Purchasing volume may be meaningful relative to the market.

Demand may continue long enough for suppliers to consider investment.

Technical requirements may be sufficiently stable for manufacturers to organise production around them.

Procurement schedules may be credible enough for suppliers to plan.

Several public buyers may also require similar products, increasing the effective demand visible to the market.

The structure of demand therefore matters alongside the amount of spending.

The European Commission's guidance on public procurement of innovation describes public buyers as capable of contributing to market creation, market shaping, and the uptake of innovative products and services.

The same general mechanism can matter for industrial capacity.

Repeated and credible purchasing can provide information about what customers are likely to require.

That information can influence what producers are willing to build.

## Suppliers Exist Inside Supply Chains

Manufacturing rarely exists in isolation.

A factory can depend on components, materials, logistics, maintenance, equipment, electricity, software, specialist skills, testing, certification, and other suppliers.

When a manufacturer expands, additional demand can therefore move through a wider production network.

A larger producer may require more components.

Component suppliers may need additional equipment.

Logistics providers may need greater capacity.

Specialist service providers may see enough demand to enter or remain in the market.

These outcomes should not be inferred from procurement policy alone.

They depend on supplier responses, financing, available production capability, infrastructure, input availability, and whether demand is sufficiently credible to influence business decisions.

The structural point is narrower.

Demand directed at final products can influence the wider system required to produce them.

## Public Demand Can Also Help Existing Capacity Survive

Industrial capacity is not only created.

It can also disappear.

Production facilities require enough economic activity to justify continued operation.

If orders remain inadequate for a prolonged period, companies may reduce output, remove equipment, close facilities, reduce workforces, or leave a market.

Rebuilding those capabilities later can require new capital, recruitment, qualification, and time.

A sufficiently predictable public customer can sometimes contribute to baseline demand that helps productive capability remain economically active.

This is a conditional effect.

It does not mean governments should preserve every supplier or every production facility.

It means demand is one of the conditions that can affect whether productive capability remains in operation.

Industrial capacity needs customers.

## Procurement Is Not the Same as Competitiveness

Supporting demand does not automatically create a competitive industry.

A supplier can receive public contracts while still facing high costs, weak productivity, technological limitations, inadequate scale, or difficulty competing in other markets.

Preference requirements can also affect competition, prices, supplier participation, and access to technologies.

These tradeoffs are part of the policy question.

The European Parliament's 2025 report supported examining a more strategic role for procurement while also emphasizing legal certainty, fair competition, value for public money, international commitments, and the avoidance of protectionist outcomes.

The distinction is important.

**Public demand can support capacity.**

It cannot substitute for competitiveness.

Manufacturers still require technology, workers, capital, infrastructure, inputs, effective operations, and customers capable of sustaining production.

Procurement can influence one part of that system.

It does not become the whole system.

## Price Is Only One Part of a Purchasing Decision

Immediate purchase price and longer term supply capability are not necessarily the same consideration.

Public procurement frameworks can allow purchasing decisions to incorporate considerations beyond initial price, depending on the applicable legal rules and procurement procedure.

Quality, innovation, sustainability, resilience, technical performance, and lifecycle characteristics can become relevant in defined circumstances.

Why does this matter for industrial capacity?

Procurement criteria influence what suppliers compete to provide.

If buyers consistently value particular characteristics, suppliers may have an economic reason to invest in capabilities needed to meet those requirements.

This can influence the direction of industrial investment.

It does not establish that investment will occur.

It also does not establish that resulting production will become internationally competitive or economically sustainable without continued public demand.

## Public Procurement as Economic Infrastructure

Traditional infrastructure enables economic activity.

A road enables movement.

A power grid enables electricity use.

A communications network enables information exchange.

Public demand operates differently.

It does not provide a physical connection between producer and customer.

It can provide another condition that industrial systems require:

**a sufficiently credible market for production.**

When demand is large enough, visible enough, and persistent enough, firms may be able to plan against it.

Production facilities can be sized against expected orders.

Suppliers can evaluate whether additional capacity is justified.

Workers can be trained for expected production.

Capital providers can assess projects against a clearer demand environment.

Supply chains may have stronger reasons to develop or maintain capability.

This is the limited sense in which procurement can sometimes function as economic infrastructure.

The government is not merely purchasing the output of an existing industrial system.

Its demand can become one of the conditions influencing whether that system is created, expanded, or maintained.

## The Limits Still Matter

Public purchasing power does not guarantee industrial development.

Procurement requirements can increase costs.

Poorly designed rules can reduce competition.

Unpredictable purchasing schedules may fail to provide enough confidence for suppliers to invest.

Large contracts can favour firms that already possess scale rather than automatically creating new competitors.

Production encouraged by public demand may also struggle if that demand later disappears and no wider market develops.

Domestic or regional preference alone therefore does not establish competitiveness.

The relevant question is whether public demand contributes to productive capability that can operate effectively, meet required standards, and remain economically viable under the conditions in which it is expected to function.

Industrial effects must therefore be assessed from observed implementation and supplier response rather than inferred directly from policy objectives.

## SINTELRA Note

Industrial capacity needs capital, infrastructure, technology, workers, materials, and operational capability.

It also needs demand.

When future demand is uncertain, producers may hesitate to build capacity that could remain unused.

When a sufficiently large and credible buyer provides sustained demand, part of that uncertainty can change.

This is why public procurement can sometimes become more than an administrative mechanism for government spending.

It can become part of the economic environment within which factories are built, suppliers invest, production systems expand, and capabilities survive.

That does not make every domestic preference productive.

It does not make protection automatically competitive.

It does not mean procurement alone creates industrial strength.

The structural point is simpler.

**Production capacity requires a market.**

Sometimes the public buyer helps create or sustain that market.

## Source Notes

### European Commission

**The Clean Industrial Deal: A joint roadmap for competitiveness and decarbonisation.**

COM(2025) 85 final.

Published 26 February 2025.

This source supports the discussion of public procurement and demand measures as potential instruments for supporting sustainable and resilient products and strengthening industrial demand conditions.

**Evidence boundary:** Policy objectives and proposed demand measures do not demonstrate that additional production capacity has been built or that procurement intervention will necessarily produce such capacity.

### European Commission

**Public Procurement of Innovation.**

Directorate General for Internal Market, Industry, Entrepreneurship and SMEs.

This source supports the discussion of public buyers as participants capable of contributing to market shaping, market creation, and uptake of innovative products and services.

**Evidence boundary:** General procurement guidance establishes potential mechanisms. It does not establish industrial capacity effects for a specific procurement programme.

### European Parliament

**Report on Public Procurement.**

A10 0147/2025.

Published 18 July 2025.

This source supports the approximately 14 percent of European Union GDP reference and the discussion of strategic procurement, resilience, industrial considerations, competition, value for public money, European preference, international commitments, and protectionism concerns.

**Evidence boundary:** The report records the European Parliament committee position and recommendations at that stage. It is not evidence that a later procurement reform has entered into force.
