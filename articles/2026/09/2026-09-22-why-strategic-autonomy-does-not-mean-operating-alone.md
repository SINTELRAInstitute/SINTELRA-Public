# Why Strategic Autonomy Does Not Mean Operating Alone

**Focus:** Strategic Autonomy • Dependency • Coordination • Infrastructure • Optionality • Resilience

## Public Question

**How can a system remain strategically autonomous while still depending on others?**

Modern economic systems are connected.

Countries import energy, materials, machinery, technology, and components.

Companies depend on suppliers, electricity networks, logistics systems, finance, data, software, and skilled workers.

Banks depend on payment and settlement infrastructure.

Digital services depend on communications networks, computing infrastructure, software, and technology providers.

Dependency is therefore a normal feature of modern systems.

The more important question is whether an essential dependency leaves enough workable choices when conditions change.

Strategic autonomy should not be confused with complete independence.

A system can remain connected to others while retaining meaningful capacity to act.

## Dependency Is Not The Same As Loss Of Autonomy

A dependency exists when an activity requires something provided by another actor, system, technology, or network.

A factory may depend on an electricity grid.

A hospital may depend on imported medical equipment.

A company may depend on external software.

A country may depend on international trade for resources that are not available domestically in sufficient quantities.

These relationships do not by themselves establish a loss of strategic autonomy.

Their importance changes when access becomes restricted, unreliable, more expensive, or unavailable.

If workable alternatives exist, the system may retain room to respond.

If alternatives are limited, the same dependency can become more consequential.

## Strategic Autonomy Is Different From Independence

Complete independence would imply that an essential function can continue without relying on another actor or external system.

Strategic autonomy is a different concept.

A system can use external suppliers while maintaining alternative sources.

It can participate in shared infrastructure while retaining other workable arrangements.

It can use foreign technology while developing internal capability or alternative technologies.

It can rely on external finance while preserving access to more than one viable financing channel.

The European Economic Security Strategy provides an institutional example of this distinction.

The strategy combines strengthening the European economic base, protecting against defined economic security risks, and partnering with countries that share relevant interests.

Its approach seeks to manage economic security risks while preserving economic openness.

Strategic autonomy in this context is therefore not equivalent to economic isolation.

## Coordination Is Different From Dependency

Coordination and dependency describe different relationships.

**Coordination means systems work together.**

**Dependency means one system requires something another system provides.**

Ports coordinate with railways.

Electricity networks coordinate across borders.

Financial institutions operate through shared payment and settlement arrangements.

Manufacturers coordinate with suppliers.

Coordination can allow systems to use shared capabilities rather than reproducing every capability independently.

A coordinated relationship can also contain dependency.

The two conditions should therefore remain conceptually separate.

## Concentration Changes The Significance Of Dependency

Dependency becomes more significant when essential supply or capability is concentrated.

A country may import an important material from several suppliers.

That is external dependency.

If most usable supply comes from a small number of sources, the available alternatives become narrower.

The same principle can apply to technology and infrastructure.

An organization may depend on cloud computing while retaining several workable providers.

Its practical options become more limited if data, software, contracts, technical architecture, or operating requirements make switching difficult.

European Union economic security and industrial policy have repeatedly connected resilience with the management of concentrated dependencies and diversification.

The relevant issue is therefore not simply whether dependency exists.

It is whether practical alternatives remain available.

## Optionality Depends On Workable Alternatives

Optionality describes the presence of meaningful choices.

A system has more practical choice when several suppliers, routes, technologies, financing channels, or operating arrangements can perform the required function.

A nominal alternative is not necessarily a usable alternative.

Another supplier may exist but lack sufficient capacity.

Another transport route may exist but be unable to carry the required volume.

Another technology may exist but be incompatible with the existing system.

Another financing source may exist but not be available for the required activity.

An alternative therefore becomes meaningful only when it can perform the required function under the relevant conditions.

This is why diversification should not be understood simply as counting alternatives.

The usability of those alternatives matters.

## Infrastructure Creates Connection

Infrastructure demonstrates why strategic autonomy does not require operating alone.

Electricity grids allow many users to obtain electricity without constructing independent power systems.

Ports connect companies to international trade.

Communications networks connect organizations and individuals.

Payment infrastructure allows financial activity to operate across institutions and markets.

Shared infrastructure creates substantial economic value because many participants can use common systems.

It also means that the continuity and resilience of those systems matter to many users simultaneously.

The Eurosystem's comprehensive payments strategy provides a current example.

Published on 31 March 2026, the strategy includes strategic autonomy and increased resilience for European payments among its four strategic aims.

At the same time, it also seeks an integrated, competitive, and innovative European payments ecosystem and continued improvement in cross border payments.

The strategy identifies excessive dependence on a small number of non European payment providers and solutions as a resilience concern.

The distinction is important.

The objective is not the elimination of every external connection.

The concern is excessive dependency where essential payment functions become too reliant on a narrow group of providers.

## Partnership Can Support Strategic Autonomy

Cooperation with other systems can expand practical choices.

International partnerships can provide access to markets, suppliers, technology, infrastructure, finance, and other capabilities that may not exist within one organization or territory.

The European Economic Security Strategy includes international partnership as one of its central pillars.

European policy in 2026 has continued to combine the reduction of strategic dependencies with diversification of trade and investment relationships.

This illustrates an important principle.

Strategic autonomy does not require every capability to exist inside a single system.

External relationships can contribute to resilience when they expand rather than narrow the range of workable options.

## Finance Provides Another Example

Modern production and infrastructure depend on capital.

Companies can obtain finance through different combinations of banks, capital markets, public institutions, investors, and other financing mechanisms.

Reliance on external finance does not by itself establish an absence of strategic freedom.

The range and usability of financing options matter.

European Council conclusions in March 2026 linked stronger resilience and reduced strategic dependencies with industrial renewal, trade and investment diversification, and deeper European capital markets.

In June 2026, the European Council again connected strategic autonomy with competitiveness, industrial renewal, innovation, resilience, investment mobilization, and reduced dependencies.

The relationship is therefore broader than simply reducing external finance.

Deeper and more diverse financing channels can themselves expand the choices available to an economic system.

## Technology Creates Less Visible Dependencies

Technology dependencies can be less visible than physical supply relationships.

An organization may control its data while depending on external software to process it.

It may operate its own infrastructure while relying on externally supplied semiconductors.

It may control an application while relying on another platform for distribution.

Industrial equipment may depend on specialized software, components, or technical support supplied elsewhere.

The European Commission's 2026 technological sovereignty package addresses several of these areas.

The package includes measures concerning semiconductors, cloud and artificial intelligence infrastructure, open source technologies, and digital infrastructure.

The Commission describes these measures in terms of strengthening technological capacity, digital autonomy, resilience, and supply security.

Again, the policy direction is not complete technological separation.

It combines stronger internal capability with measures intended to increase resilience and reduce excessive dependency.

## Dependencies Can Be Managed In Different Ways

A system does not necessarily need to eliminate a dependency in order to reduce its exposure.

Depending on the function involved, possible responses can include:

• diversifying suppliers

• maintaining inventories

• developing substitute technologies

• expanding infrastructure

• preserving internal technical capability

• creating additional financing channels

• establishing partnerships

• improving information about important dependencies

• increasing contractual flexibility

• developing contingency arrangements

Different dependencies require different responses.

A measure appropriate for an imported material may not be appropriate for software, finance, electricity infrastructure, or payment systems.

The appropriate response depends on the function that must continue and the practical alternatives available.

## Not Every Dependency Is Strategic

Modern systems depend on very large numbers of external goods and services.

Not all dependencies have the same significance.

A dependency becomes more strategically important when it affects a function considered important and practical alternatives are limited.

European Commission work on strategic dependencies has similarly distinguished ordinary dependencies from dependencies associated with strategically important areas and limited alternatives.

Context therefore matters.

The relevant public question is not simply:

**Does a dependency exist?**

It is:

**How important is the function, and what practical choices remain if access changes?**

## Efficiency And Strategic Autonomy Are Not Identical

Concentration can emerge because a particular supplier, route, technology, or financing arrangement is efficient.

Using one highly efficient system can reduce costs and simplify operations.

But efficiency under normal conditions does not automatically establish resilience under changed conditions.

A system can be highly efficient while having few practical alternatives.

Conversely, maintaining alternatives can involve additional cost, duplication, or complexity.

Strategic autonomy and operating efficiency therefore describe different characteristics of a system.

Policy and business decisions can involve tradeoffs between them.

## Complete Self Sufficiency Also Has Costs

Attempting to eliminate every external dependency would create substantial economic and practical costs.

Some countries do not possess particular natural resources.

Some technologies depend on knowledge and production distributed across several countries.

Some infrastructure creates value precisely because it connects many users and markets.

International specialization can support competition, innovation, scale, and access to capabilities that would be expensive to reproduce independently.

This is why strategic autonomy should not automatically be interpreted as self sufficiency.

A more practical question is whether connection preserves or narrows the ability to respond when circumstances change.

## Strategic Autonomy Changes Over Time

Strategic autonomy is not necessarily permanent.

Available choices can expand or contract.

A new supplier can add another source.

A new transport connection can create another route.

A compatible technical standard can make substitution easier.

A new financing channel can expand capital access.

Conversely, consolidation can reduce the number of suppliers.

Technical incompatibility can make switching harder.

The loss of internal skills can increase reliance on external technical support.

The relevant conditions can therefore change even when the underlying dependency remains.

## Strategic Autonomy Is About Preserving Meaningful Choice

The broad relationship can be expressed publicly as:

**Connection → Dependency Awareness → Alternatives → Coordination → Practical Choice**

This is not a scoring system or a universal sequence.

It is a simple way to understand why connection and autonomy can coexist.

A connected system can retain strategic autonomy when important dependencies are understood and workable alternatives remain available.

A system becomes more constrained when essential functions depend on arrangements for which practical alternatives are limited.

## SINTELRA Note

Strategic autonomy does not require operating alone.

Modern infrastructure, finance, technology, trade, and production systems are interconnected.

Dependency is therefore part of normal economic and institutional activity.

The important distinction is between being connected and having too few workable choices.

External suppliers, infrastructure, finance, technology, and international partners can support economic activity while a system retains meaningful capacity to respond to changing conditions.

Concentrated dependencies become more consequential when essential functions have limited practical alternatives.

The objective is therefore not necessarily to eliminate connection.

It is to preserve meaningful choice while remaining connected.
