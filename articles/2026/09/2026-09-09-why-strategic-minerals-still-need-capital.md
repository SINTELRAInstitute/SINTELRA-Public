# Why Strategic Minerals Still Need Capital

**Repository:** `SINTELRA-Public`

**Access:** Public

**Date:** 09 September 2026

**Focus:** Critical Minerals • Strategic Projects • Financing • Industrial Capacity • Resource Development • Supply Capacity

## Public Question

**Why can a strategically important mineral project still fail to become actual supply?**

Calling a mineral strategic does not produce another tonne of it.

Neither does identifying a promising deposit, approving a project, or placing that project on a government priority list.

All of those steps can matter.

But physical supply appears only after something much harder happens.

A project has to become executable.

The European Union's Strategic Projects program under the Critical Raw Materials Act provides a useful way to see the difference.

## Strategic Importance Is the Beginning

The European Union has identified a group of raw materials as strategically important because they are needed across industries such as energy, digital technology, aerospace, and defence.

Under the Critical Raw Materials Act, the European Commission can recognise extraction, processing, recycling, and substitution projects as Strategic Projects when they meet defined criteria, including contribution to supply security, technical feasibility, sustainability, and sufficiently credible expected production volumes.

The first selection in 2025 included 47 Strategic Projects inside the European Union. Another 13 projects outside the Union were subsequently selected. A second application round closed in January 2026 after the Commission received more than 160 applications.

Recognition matters because it can give a project greater institutional support.

It does not mean the material is already being produced.

That distinction is fundamental.

## A Project Is Not Yet Supply

Consider what has to happen between identifying a strategically useful mineral resource and delivering usable material to industry.

A simplified sequence looks like this:

**Strategic importance → Project development → Financing → Permits → Infrastructure → Construction → Commissioning → Production → Processing → Usable material**

Every step requires something different.

A geological resource may exist, but a project still needs engineering.

An engineering plan may exist, but the developer still needs money.

Financing may be available, but permits may still be required.

Permits may be secured, but roads, power, water, processing equipment, waste systems, and other infrastructure may still need to be built.

Construction may finish, but the facility still has to start operating reliably.

Only then does projected capacity begin turning into actual supply.

## Capital Connects the Plan to the Physical System

Mineral projects can require large amounts of capital before meaningful production begins.

Money may be needed for geological work, engineering studies, environmental assessment, equipment, processing facilities, electricity connections, transport infrastructure, construction, workforce development, and commissioning.

This is one reason the financing question remains important even after a project has been designated as strategic.

When the European Commission selected the first 47 Strategic Projects inside the EU in March 2025, it estimated that they would require about **€22.5 billion in total capital investment to become operational**.

That number illustrates the difference between recognising capacity that Europe wants and creating capacity that physically exists.

Strategic status can improve the path.

Capital still has to cross it.

## Strategic Status Does Not Equal Automatic Funding

The Critical Raw Materials Act explicitly includes mechanisms to help Strategic Projects gain access to finance.

Project promoters can request support from a financing subgroup that brings together institutions including national promotional banks, the European Investment Bank, the European Bank for Reconstruction and Development, and private financial institutions. The purpose is to discuss how financing might be completed using available private and public sources.

That is significant support.

But it is not the same as automatic funding.

A project still needs a financing structure that works.

Banks, public institutions, companies, and other capital providers still have to assess costs, risks, expected revenues, technical maturity, construction requirements, and the project's ability to operate over time.

Public finance can sometimes help address financing gaps that private capital is unwilling or unable to carry alone. The form and availability of that support depend on the relevant financing programme and the project itself.

But public money does not remove the physical requirements of building the project.

Nor does strategic importance automatically make every project economically executable.

## Permits Are Another Form of Capacity

Money alone is also insufficient.

Mineral development affects land, water, communities, infrastructure, ecosystems, and industrial systems. Projects therefore operate within environmental, safety, planning, and other regulatory requirements.

The Critical Raw Materials Act gives Strategic Projects inside the EU access to more predictable permitting procedures. The regulation generally limits the permit granting process to 27 months for Strategic Projects involving extraction and 15 months for projects involving only processing or recycling, while providing defined exclusions and possible extensions under specified circumstances.

Faster and more predictable procedures can reduce uncertainty.

They do not eliminate permitting.

Strategic Project recognition also does not override the legal requirements that remain applicable to the project.

This matters because industrial capacity cannot be separated from institutional capacity.

A project must be financeable, technically buildable, legally permitted, and operationally workable.

## Then Something Still Has to Be Built

After finance and permits comes the physical system.

A mineral project may require a mine, processing plant, recycling facility, electricity supply, transport connections, water systems, storage, waste management, specialized machinery, and connections to customers further along the industrial chain.

These components take time and capital to construct.

They also depend on one another.

A processing facility without reliable electricity is not full industrial capacity.

A mine without sufficient transport access may struggle to move material.

A resource without processing capacity may not produce the form of material that manufacturers actually need.

The important unit is therefore not simply the mineral deposit.

It is the functioning production system around it.

## Production Capacity Must Become Actual Production

There is another distinction after construction.

A facility designed to produce a certain amount of material does not necessarily produce that amount immediately.

New industrial facilities usually have to be commissioned, tested, stabilized, and gradually brought toward their intended operating level.

Equipment must work together.

Workers need operating procedures.

Input materials need to arrive.

Quality standards need to be achieved.

Customers need material in usable specifications.

This creates an important public distinction:

**Planned capacity is not constructed capacity.**

**Constructed capacity is not operating capacity.**

**Operating capacity is not necessarily actual output.**

This is why project announcements should not be counted as completed mineral supply.

They describe intended future capability.

Actual supply has to be produced.

## The Financing Challenge Starts Even Earlier

The capital requirement does not begin only when construction starts.

Exploration itself requires funding.

A July 2026 study commissioned by the European Investment Bank concluded that decades of underinvestment had left Europe with too few exploration projects ready to progress toward mining. It estimated that annual EU exploration spending would need to rise from roughly €0.2 billion to around €2 billion over the following five years to help meet the Union's domestic extraction objectives.

This exposes a longer development chain.

Europe does not only need capital for mines that are nearly ready to build.

It also needs earlier capital to generate the projects that may become buildable later.

Supply capacity therefore has a pipeline.

If too little activity enters the beginning of that pipeline, later policy goals can face a shortage of mature projects regardless of how strategically important the minerals have become.

## Strategic Policy Can Improve the Conditions

None of this means that financing constraints prove that strategic minerals policy has failed.

The purpose of identifying Strategic Projects is partly to improve the conditions under which important projects can progress.

The EU framework provides support for financing coordination, more predictable permitting, contacts with potential buyers, and institutional attention.

Those measures can reduce obstacles.

They cannot replace execution.

Similarly, public financing can help close specific gaps, especially where commercially difficult projects also provide broader strategic value.

But public capital is still capital.

It must eventually support something technically viable enough to build and operate.

The strategic label can change the priority given to a project.

It cannot change the laws of construction.

## From Strategic Resource to Supply Capacity

The core distinction is simple.

A strategic mineral is something an economy considers important.

Strategic supply capacity is the physical and institutional ability to deliver that mineral in usable form.

The two are connected, but they are not identical.

Moving from one to the other requires several forms of capacity:

1. **Resource knowledge:** Knowing what resource exists.
2. **Project capability:** Developing a technically credible way to extract, process, or recycle it.
3. **Financial capacity:** Securing enough capital to proceed.
4. **Institutional capacity:** Completing the required permitting and regulatory processes.
5. **Infrastructure capacity:** Providing the power, transport, water, facilities, and equipment needed for operation.
6. **Construction capacity:** Turning designs and financing into physical assets.
7. **Operating capacity:** Making the completed system work reliably.
8. **Production capacity:** Delivering usable material at meaningful scale.

A weakness at any one of these stages can delay the final result.

That is why strategic importance alone cannot create supply.

## SINTELRA Note

Critical mineral policy often begins with a question of importance:

**Which materials does the economy need?**

Physical capacity begins with a different question:

**What must exist for those materials actually to be produced?**

The answer includes geological knowledge, capital, permits, infrastructure, construction, processing, people, and operating capability.

Strategic designation can help organize attention around that problem.

Financing can help mobilize the resources needed to solve it.

Neither should be confused with the final outcome.

A project becomes supply only when the full system required to produce usable material is functioning.

The resource may be strategic.

The capacity still has to be built.

## Source Notes

The European Commission's current Strategic Projects framework under the Critical Raw Materials Act defines the purpose and qualification criteria for Strategic Projects and records the first approved projects.

Regulation (EU) 2024/1252 establishes the legal framework for Strategic Projects, including technical feasibility requirements, financing coordination, permitting provisions, and continued compliance with applicable law.

The European Commission reported that the first 47 Strategic Projects inside the EU were expected to require approximately €22.5 billion in capital investment to become operational.

The European Commission's Strategic Projects FAQ explains that status provides access to financing coordination and potential public and private financing sources rather than automatic project funding.

The European Investment Bank reported in July 2026 that greater exploration investment would also be required to develop a larger pipeline of European raw material projects.
