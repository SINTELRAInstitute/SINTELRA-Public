# When Industrial Capacity Becomes Difficult to Keep

**Focus:** Industrial Capacity • Utilization • Production Cost • Competition • Demand • Capacity Retention

## Public Question

**What keeps industrial capacity alive after the factories have already been built?**

Building a factory creates physical capacity.

Keeping that factory economically usable is a different problem.

A production site can still contain machinery, workers, utilities, storage, and transport connections while producing far below the level it was designed to handle.

If that condition lasts long enough, the existence of the factory does not guarantee the survival of the capacity.

This distinction is visible in parts of Europe's chemicals and plastics industries.

Current evidence points to pressure from weak demand, high energy and feedstock costs, low utilization, and international competition. These factors do not mean that every European plant faces the same conditions. They do show why industrial capacity can weaken even after the physical assets have already been built.

## Physical Capacity Is Not the Same as Usable Capacity

Imagine a factory designed to produce one million units each year.

The equipment remains capable of producing that amount.

But customers order only 650,000 units.

The factory still exists.

Its practical economic position has changed.

Many industrial costs continue even when output falls. A company may still need maintenance, specialist staff, safety systems, energy connections, compliance systems, buildings, logistics infrastructure, and other fixed capabilities.

When fewer products move through the same industrial system, those costs have to be supported by less output.

That can make every unit more difficult to produce competitively.

This is why capacity utilization matters.

It describes how much of available production capability is actually being used.

A plant can therefore retain its physical capacity while losing economically sustainable utilization.

## The Current European Chemicals Case

The European Commission identified this problem in its European Chemicals Industry Action Plan.

The Commission stated that high energy and feedstock prices, geopolitical pressures, and low market demand had weakened the competitiveness of European producers and contributed to declining production utilization. It also reported that more than 20 major production site closures had been announced in the European Union during the preceding two years.

The problem has remained visible in 2026.

Cefic, the European chemical industry association, reported in June 2026 that EU chemical capacity utilization was around 74 percent, which it described as historically low and below its longer term level. Its report also stated that chemical production in the first quarter of 2026 was 3.2 percent below the same period a year earlier.

These figures do not show that all European chemical capacity is uneconomic.

They show that a substantial amount of installed production capability is operating below the level historically associated with the sector.

That gap matters.

## Plastics Show the Same Structural Problem

Plastics production provides another example.

A Plastics Europe report published in August 2026, using Eurostat, Macrobond, and PED data, reported that EU production of plastics in primary forms declined by 1.6 percent in the first quarter of 2026 compared with the previous quarter and by 8.5 percent compared with the same quarter a year earlier.

The report linked the difficult environment to weak demand together with rising energy and feedstock costs.

Again, the important point is not that Europe's plastics factories suddenly disappeared.

Much of the production infrastructure remained physically present.

The problem was that less output was moving through it while important input costs remained under pressure.

That is how installed capacity and economically usable capacity can begin to separate.

## Utilization Connects Demand to Industrial Survival

Factories are built to produce.

They do not remain economically healthy merely because the machinery still works.

Demand determines how much of that machinery is used.

When customer industries expand, factories can increase production and spread many operating costs across more units.

When demand weakens, the process can move in the opposite direction.

A simple sequence looks like this:

**Weaker demand → Lower production → Lower capacity utilization → Greater cost pressure per unit → Reduced competitiveness → Pressure on continued operation**

The sequence is not automatic.

Some facilities have lower fixed costs.

Some produce specialized products with stronger margins.

Some can reduce production efficiently.

Others serve customers that remain relatively stable.

But utilization helps explain why a technically functional plant can become economically difficult to maintain.

## Production Costs Matter Even After Construction Is Finished

Construction cost receives substantial attention when new industrial capacity is discussed.

Operating cost becomes more important after the facility has been built.

Chemicals and plastics production can require large quantities of energy and feedstocks.

The European Commission identifies energy cost as particularly important for energy intensive industries and notes that energy costs influence industrial investment decisions.

For chemicals, the issue is broader because fossil resources can serve both as energy and as production inputs. The Commission's Chemicals Industry Action Plan notes this dual dependence on energy and feedstock.

A factory therefore does not become economically secure when construction ends.

It must repeatedly purchase the inputs required to operate.

If those inputs remain expensive relative to competing production locations, an existing facility can become less competitive even though nothing is physically wrong with the plant.

## Competition Changes the Value of Existing Capacity

A factory also operates inside a market.

Customers can often choose among suppliers from several regions.

If another producer can deliver a comparable product at a lower total cost, the existence of domestic production capacity does not guarantee that customers will use it.

Competition can come from differences in energy cost, feedstock availability, manufacturing scale, logistics, technology, productivity, financing conditions, or other production advantages.

Cefic reported that European natural gas prices during the first four months of 2026 were about 3.3 times United States levels and identified this cost difference as a continuing pressure on energy intensive chemical production.

This does not establish the competitiveness of every individual European product.

It illustrates how production location can affect the economics of using installed capacity.

Imported products become relevant to capacity retention when external suppliers can serve the same market under more competitive conditions.

The question then becomes not whether the domestic factory exists.

The question becomes whether customers continue to provide enough economic demand to keep it operating.

## Low Utilization Can Reinforce the Problem

Capacity pressure can become self reinforcing.

Consider a plant operating well below its intended scale.

The facility still carries maintenance and organizational costs.

Lower output can make those costs more difficult to absorb.

The plant may then become less competitive.

If customers shift further toward alternative suppliers, utilization can fall again.

The sequence can become:

**Lower demand → Lower utilization → Higher effective production burden → Weaker competitive position → Further pressure on demand**

This does not happen in every industry or every facility.

But it explains why unused capacity cannot always simply wait indefinitely for demand to return.

Industrial systems have carrying costs.

## Capacity Can Move Through Several States

Industrial capacity should not be treated as a simple choice between existing and nonexistent.

It can move through several practical states.

1. **Installed capacity:** The physical production assets exist.
2. **Available capacity:** The facility is technically capable of operating.
3. **Utilized capacity:** Part of that capability is actually being used.
4. **Economically sustainable capacity:** Production generates sufficient economic value to justify continued operation.
5. **Reduced or idle capacity:** Production is deliberately lowered or stopped for a period.
6. **Closed capacity:** The facility is no longer maintained as an operating production asset.

These states are different.

A factory can move from high utilization to low utilization without disappearing.

It can remain idle while retaining some possibility of restarting.

It can also reach a point where maintaining or restarting the asset is no longer considered economically justified.

This is why industrial capacity can decline before all the physical structures disappear.

## Industrial Ecosystems Make Retention More Complicated

Large industrial facilities rarely operate alone.

Chemical and plastics plants often belong to industrial clusters connected through energy systems, pipelines, feedstocks, logistics, shared services, and exchanges of intermediate products.

The European Commission has noted that closures of upstream chemical facilities can create effects across polymers, plastics, specialty chemicals, and other parts of the chemical ecosystem.

This creates another capacity problem.

The loss of one facility can change the economics of another.

A downstream producer may lose a nearby input supplier.

Transport requirements may increase.

Shared infrastructure may have fewer users.

An industrial cluster can therefore contain interdependent capacity.

Retention is not always a factory by factory question.

Sometimes it is a system question.

## Protecting Capacity Is Not the Same as Making It Competitive

The existence of industrial pressure does not establish one specific policy response.

Keeping a factory open does not automatically improve productivity.

Protecting a producer from competition does not automatically reduce its production costs.

Financial support does not automatically create long term demand.

Tariffs, subsidies, energy measures, procurement rules, regulatory changes, and infrastructure investment each affect different parts of the system and can produce different effects under different conditions.

This article does not evaluate those instruments.

The structural distinction is enough.

**Retaining physical capacity and retaining competitive capacity are not the same task.**

Industrial strength requires more than preventing assets from closing.

Those assets must also remain capable of producing goods that customers will continue to buy under economically workable conditions.

## What Keeps Capacity Alive?

Factories need more than machines.

They need inputs that can be obtained at workable costs.

They need enough demand to use the equipment.

They need workers and technical capability.

They need functioning infrastructure.

They need access to customers.

They need enough revenue to maintain assets and finance future investment.

And they need a competitive position that makes continued production rational.

The resulting structure looks like this:

**Physical assets → Operating inputs → Demand → Utilization → Competitive production → Revenue → Maintenance and reinvestment → Capacity retention**

Weakness in one part of this chain does not automatically destroy a factory.

Persistent weakness across several parts can make capacity progressively harder to retain.

## SINTELRA Note

Industrial capacity is often discussed as something that countries either possess or lack.

The reality is more dynamic.

A factory can exist physically while producing less.

A production line can remain installed while orders disappear.

Technical capability can remain available while operating economics deteriorate.

Eventually, capacity that is rarely used and difficult to operate competitively can stop functioning as meaningful industrial capacity.

Building factories is therefore only one part of industrial strength.

The other part is maintaining the economic conditions that allow those factories to keep producing.

The asset creates potential capacity.

Utilization turns that potential into production.

Economic viability helps keep that capacity alive.
